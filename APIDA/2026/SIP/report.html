SIP Grant: Data Exploration

Spring 2025

About the Analyses

In this report, student metrics were calculated for the SIP grant. The analyses include GPRA measures:

  • Total fall enrollment
  • Fall-to-fall retention
  • 100% time to graduation rate (2 years)
  • 150% time to graduation rate (3 years)

These metrics were disaggregated based on the following demographics:

  • STEM or non-STEM majors: This was determined based on students’ last major on file, regardless of the degree or certificate they were awarded.
  • Low-income status: Students were categorized as low-income if they were eligible for a Pell Grant during the academic year.
  • Race/ethnicity: Students were categorized inclusively into racial and ethnic categories based on their self-reported data.
  • Gender identity: Students were categorized based on their self-reported gender identity.

Time to Graduation Rate

Overall time to graduation rates reflect the percentage of first-time students at Fullerton College who completed a degree or certificate within the District withintwo or three years after initial enrollment. These rates are based on cohort tracking, following students who began in a given fall term over time. In this analysis, cohorts include students who started in Fall 2020 through Fall 2022. Graduation rates are reported at 100% time (2 years) and 150% time (3 years).

STEM vs. Non-STEM Majors

Time to graduation rates were also disaggregated based on students’ last major on file, categorized as STEM or non-STEM.

Overall, students with STEM majors had lower graduation rates at both 100% time (2 years) and 150% time (3 years) compared to students with non-STEM majors. Students with non-STEM majors were completing their awards at nearly double the rate of students with STEM majors within two years. The gap in graduation rates between STEM and non-STEM majors narrowed at three years, but students with non-STEM majors were still graduating at a higher rate than students with STEM majors.

Low-Income Status

Time to graduation rates were also disaggregated based on low-income status, operationalized as those students who qualified for federal Pell Grant assistance during the academic year.

Overall, low-income students had lower graduation rates at both 100% time (2 years) and 150% time (3 years) compared to non-low-income students. The gap in graduation rates between low-income and non-low-income students narrowed at three years. However, non-low-income students were still graduating at a slightly higher rate than low-income students at three years.

Intersection of STEM vs. Non-STEM Majors and Low-Income Status

To further investigate the intersection of STEM vs. non-STEM majors and low-income status, time to graduation rates were disaggregated based on both students’ last major on file (STEM vs. non-STEM) and low-income status (Pell Grant eligibility).

100% Time (2-Year) to Graduation Rates

Graduation rates at 100% time (2-years) to completion differ by both income status and major type. Within STEM, rates are relatively low for both groups, with non-low-income students slightly outperforming low-income students. This suggests that while income matters, early completion in STEM is limited overall.

In non-STEM majors, rates are higher across the board, and the income gap is more pronounced. Non-low-income students have the highest outcomes, while low-income students still perform better than their STEM counterparts. Overall, non-STEM pathways show stronger short-term completion, with income status widening those differences.

150% Time (3-Year) to Graduation Rates

At 150% time to completion, graduation rates increase for all groups, and differences by income and major become smaller. Within STEM, the pattern is mixed rather than consistent. In some cohorts, low-income students have higher completion rates, while in others, non-low-income students do. Overall rates are very similar between the two groups, suggesting no clear or stable income status advantage in STEM at 150% time.

In non-STEM majors, rates remain slightly higher overall, with not low-income students continuing to have a small advantage. Overall, extended time to completion appears to reduce differences, particularly within STEM, while non-STEM pathways still show somewhat higher completion levels.
